A pasni and a bratabandha each happen once in a child's life. They're shorter than a wedding, but they have their own rituals, in their own order, and the important ones are quick. Here's what to plan for.

## Pasni: the first rice

The pasni (annaprashan) is a baby's first taste of rice, usually held in the fifth month for a girl and the sixth for a boy, on a day the family priest picks. Customs differ between families and communities, but these are the moments families most often ask for:

- **Dressing the baby.** Often in new clothes and jewellery given by the maternal family.
- **The first feeding.** Usually rice pudding (kheer), fed by an elder of the family, and then by each relative in turn.
- **Tika and blessings** from grandparents and elders.
- **Choosing from the tray.** Many families place objects in front of the baby (a book, a pen, money, clay, jewellery) and watch which one the baby reaches for. It's the moment everyone crowds around, so tell the photographer before it starts.
- **Family groups:** the baby with parents, with grandparents on both sides, and with the maternal family.

### Plan around the baby

The baby decides how the day goes. Try to time the ceremony after a nap and a feed, keep the room from getting too hot and crowded, and don't worry about tears: some of the best pasni photos are of a baby who clearly did not want the rice.

## Bratabandha: the sacred thread

In a bratabandha, a boy receives the sacred thread (janai) and is initiated into adulthood in the family's tradition, usually somewhere between the ages of seven and thirteen. It's a long day, and much of it happens early in the morning. The moments that make the strongest photos:

- **The shaving of the head,** leaving the tuft of hair (tupi). Often at dawn, so the photographer needs to arrive early.
- **The saffron robes** and the walking stick of an ascetic.
- **Receiving the janai** and the teaching from the guru, often whispered into the boy's ear under a cloth.
- **The bhiksha round,** when he asks his relatives for alms, starting with his mother.
- **Leaving and coming back.** In many families, the boy sets off as if to leave home for a life of study, and his mama (maternal uncle) brings him back. Families love this moment and it's easy to miss.
- **Family groups** with the boy in his robes, especially with his maternal family.

## For both ceremonies

- **Share the timings from the priest** a few days before, including anything that happens before sunrise.
- **Keep one relative free** to gather people for group photos. The elders are the ones you'll most want pictures with later.
- **Leave room around the ritual.** Relatives with phones tend to form a wall in front of the child at the key moments. Ask them to give the photographer a gap for those few seconds.

## Afterwards

Pasni and bratabandha photos make good small [photo books](/prints/photo-books) to send to relatives abroad, and a single framed portrait of the child from the day often stays on the wall for years.
